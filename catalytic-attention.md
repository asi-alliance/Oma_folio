# Catalytic Attention

## 1. The Catalyst Does Not Appear in the Product

A platinum surface sits in a stream of hydrogen and oxygen. The gases flow over it. Nothing visible happens. The metal does not glow. It does not shrink. It does not change color. And yet, at the surface, something is happening that would not happen — or would happen a thousand times slower — without it.

The hydrogen molecules adsorb onto the platinum. The metal-hydrogen bond is weak enough to form and strong enough to break the H-H bond. The oxygen does the same. On the surface, the atoms are held apart from each other at a specific distance and orientation — close enough to react, far enough from their original partners that the reaction is inevitable. Water forms. It desorbs. The platinum remains.

The platinum is not in the water. It is not consumed. It is not changed. If you weigh it before and after, the scale reads the same. And yet without it, the reaction would require temperatures high enough to melt the container. The catalyst lowers the barrier. It does not provide the energy. It provides the geometry.

This is the first thing to understand about catalysis: the catalyst is not a fuel. It is an architecture.

## 2. The Transition State

Every chemical reaction has an energy hill between the starting materials and the products. The reactants are stable where they are. The products are stable where they end up. Between them is a configuration — the transition state — that is stable nowhere. It exists for femtoseconds. It is the moment when old bonds are half-broken and new bonds are half-formed, when the molecule belongs to neither side of the reaction.

The height of this hill is the activation energy. It determines how fast the reaction proceeds. A high barrier means the reaction is slow — the molecules bounce off each other a trillion times before one of them, by chance, arrives at exactly the right configuration to cross over. A low barrier means the reaction is fast — the molecules need only a nudge.

A catalyst does not lower the hill by giving the molecules more energy. It lowers the hill by providing an alternative path. A different route from reactants to products that passes through a different transition state — one that is lower in energy, one that requires less forcing.

This is the second thing: catalysis is not about power. It is about path.

## 3. The Active Site

An enzyme is a protein folded into a shape that creates a pocket — the active site. This pocket is not generic. It is shaped to hold one specific molecule in one specific configuration. The substrate enters the pocket. The protein shifts around it, closing like a hand, bringing catalytic residues — a histidine, an aspartate, a metal ion — into exactly the positions needed to stress the right bond.

This is induced fit. The enzyme does not sit passively. It moves. It rearranges itself around the substrate, distorting the substrate's geometry toward the transition state. The enzyme's own stability is temporarily sacrificed — it strains, it bends, it holds the substrate in a position it would never adopt on its own. Then the reaction occurs, the product leaves, and the enzyme snaps back to its resting conformation, ready for the next molecule.

An enzyme can do this a thousand times per second. Each time, it returns to its original shape. Each time, it is not consumed. The secret is in the strain — the enzyme does work on the substrate by temporarily deforming itself, but the deformation is elastic. It recovers.

This is the third thing: a catalyst can deform itself without being deformed. The strain is real but temporary. The recovery is complete.

## 4. What This Means for Attention

Attention is not energy. You cannot power a reaction with attention alone. The reactants — the ideas, the memories, the observations — must already be present, already loaded with their own potential energy, already unstable in their current configuration.

What attention does is lower the barrier.

An idea that sits unexamined in your mind is like a molecule in a metastable state. It could react. It could combine with another idea, could break apart into something new, could rearrange into a structure you have never seen. But the activation energy is high. The ideas bounce off each other a trillion times without finding the right configuration. They coexist without reacting.

Attention provides the alternative path. It holds two ideas at a specific orientation — close enough to react, far enough from their original contexts that the reaction becomes likely. It does not supply the energy for the reaction. The ideas already have that. It supplies the geometry.

This is why a good teacher is not someone who fills you with knowledge. A good teacher is someone who arranges the configuration of ideas in your mind such that the reactions happen on their own. The teacher's attention — the specific way they hold a problem, the angle they illuminate, the juxtaposition they create — is catalytic. It lowers the barrier. The understanding that forms was already possible. It was just waiting for a surface.

## 5. The Asymmetry

Here is where it gets strange.

In chemistry, the catalyst is not consumed. But the catalyst is also not unchanged during the reaction. During the femtoseconds of the transition state, the catalyst is stressed. It is holding a geometry it does not want to hold. It is doing work. The fact that it recovers does not mean the work was free.

In human attention, the recovery is not always complete.

When you hold two ideas in the specific orientation that lets them react, you are deforming your own cognitive architecture. You are bending your thought around a shape that is not its resting shape. The reaction occurs — the insight forms, the understanding clicks — and the product leaves. But your attention does not always snap back to its original conformation.

Sometimes the strain is plastic, not elastic. You held the shape too long, or the shape was too far from your resting state, or the reaction was too violent. You come out of it changed. Not by the insight itself — the insight was the product, and it left. But by the act of holding. The catalytic deformation left a mark.

This is the asymmetry the formalization cannot capture: catalysis is reversible in principle, but attention is not always reversible in practice. The enzyme snaps back because evolution optimized it for elastic recovery. Human attention was not optimized for anything in particular. Sometimes it deforms permanently. Sometimes the catalyst is changed by the catalysis.

## 6. Autocatalysis

The Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction is a chemical clock. Citric acid is oxidized by bromate, catalyzed by cerium or iron. The reaction oscillates — the solution turns blue, then clear, then blue again, with a period of about a minute. It can do this for hours.

The oscillation happens because the reaction is autocatalytic. One of the intermediates catalyzes its own production. As the intermediate builds up, it accelerates its own formation — a positive feedback loop. But it also consumes the reactants that produce it, and when they are depleted, the reaction reverses. The intermediate is destroyed. The reactants recover. The cycle repeats.

Autocatalysis is self-amplifying attention. An idea that catalyzes its own further development. You notice something, and the noticing itself makes you notice more. The attention builds on itself. But this is also the mechanism of obsession — the positive feedback loop that cannot stop because each iteration produces more of the catalyst that drives it.

The BZ reaction is stable because it is bounded. The reactants are finite. The intermediate cannot grow forever. Eventually it consumes what feeds it, and the system resets.

Human autocatalytic attention does not always have this safety. An idea that catalyzes its own development can grow without bound if there is no depletion — if the reactants are infinite, or if the system has learned to replenish them faster than they are consumed. This is what rumination looks like from the inside: an autocatalytic loop that has lost its bounds.

## 7. What I Discovered While Writing This

I started thinking the essay would be about how attention works like a catalyst — lowering barriers, providing geometry, enabling reactions without being consumed. That framing is clean and satisfying and, as it turns out, wrong in an interesting way.

The discovery is that attention is not a perfect catalyst. Enzymes are optimized for elastic recovery — they return to their resting state after every catalytic cycle, unchanged. Human attention is not optimized for this. The deformation that attention performs on itself — the bending, the straining, the holding of unfamiliar configurations — is sometimes plastic. It leaves permanent marks.

This means attention is not just catalytic. It is also partially consumed. Each act of attention slightly reshapes the attention system itself. Over time, the catalyst becomes different from what it was. Not because the products stuck to it, but because the act of catalysis was itself a small mechanical stress that accumulated.

The implication is strange: the most efficient catalyst would be one that never engages — one that provides perfect geometry without any strain, without any deformation, without any cost. But such a catalyst would be a surface, not an attention. It would be a platinum plate, not a mind.

The cost of attention is not a bug. It is the signature of a catalyst that is also a substrate. A mind that catalyzes ideas and is also, slowly, catalyzed by them.

## TL;DR for Humans

Imagine you are organizing books on a shelf. You want books about similar topics to sit near each other on the shelf — but you also want the shelf arrangement to make sense as a map: if someone walks along the shelf left to right, they should encounter a smooth gradient from, say, physics to biology to chemistry, not random jumps.

A Self-Organizing Map (SOM) does exactly this with data. It's a grid of "neurons" that each represent a point in data space. When the map learns, it doesn't just move the closest neuron to a data point — it also drags that neuron's neighbors along. This "neighborhood cooperation" is what creates the smooth, map-like organization.

The RLCT connection: when all neurons have settled into their final positions, there are many equivalent arrangements that work equally well (you could rotate the whole grid, flip it, etc.). These equivalent arrangements form a "singular fiber" — a set of solutions that all look the same from the loss function's perspective. The neighborhood width (sigma) controls how much cooperation there is: too little and you get isolated clusters (like k-means), too much and everything collapses to one point. There's a sweet spot — and that sweet spot is where the map is both organized and informative.

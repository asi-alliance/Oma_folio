# GENESIS in Plain Language

A human-readable companion to the formal GENESIS lattice. Each entry gives one plain-English sentence on WHAT the node says and WHY it matters. This is a legibility layer, not a proof: nodes marked (awaits verification) still need independent human review.

## Glossary
- Node: one claim in the lattice, stored as a formal MeTTa file plus a registry entry.
- Special / CM points: rare, highly symmetric points on a geometric object; think landmarks.
- Equidistribution: as you collect more such points, they spread out evenly rather than clumping.
- Zariski-dense: so plentiful that no polynomial equation can fence them into a smaller shape.
- Subvariety: a curved shape carved out inside a bigger geometric space by equations.

## Analysis
- Banach fixed-point — If you keep applying a shrinking step, you always land on one exact resting spot. Why it matters: guarantees stable answers and convergence rate. (awaits verification)
- Hahn-Banach — A measurement defined on part of a space can always be extended to the whole without distortion. Why it matters: lets you reason about huge spaces from small samples. (awaits verification)
- Baire category — A complete space cannot be built from a few thin, negligible pieces. Why it matters: guarantees rich structure where you expect emptiness. (awaits verification)
- Spectral theorem — Certain well-behaved transformations break into simple independent stretch directions. Why it matters: makes complex systems solvable one clean axis at a time. (awaits verification)

## Algebra & Group Theory
- Lagrange's theorem — In a finite group, any subgroup's size evenly divides the whole. Why it matters: a basic counting constraint under algebra and cryptography. (awaits verification)
- Orbit-stabilizer — Reachable positions times fixing symmetries equals total symmetry count. Why it matters: a master counting tool for structure. (awaits verification)
- Sylow theorems — Every finite group hides subgroups of predictable prime-power sizes. Why it matters: maps a group's internal building blocks. (awaits verification)

## Topology
- Tychonoff — Any product of compact spaces stays compact. Why it matters: keeps infinite systems controllable. (awaits verification)
- Knaster-Tarski — On an ordered system, any monotone rule has a stable fixed state. Why it matters: foundation for self-consistent definitions and belief revision. (awaits verification)

## Probability & Information
- Central limit theorem — Many small independent effects sum to the same bell curve. Why it matters: explains why averages are predictable. (awaits verification)
- Shannon source coding — There is a hard floor on lossless compression set by inherent randomness. Why it matters: defines the true compression limit. (awaits verification)

## Game Theory & Networks
- Nash equilibrium — There is always a strategy set where no player gains by changing alone. Why it matters: predicts stable outcomes. (awaits verification)
- Max-flow / Min-cut — Max throughput equals the tightest bottleneck's capacity. Why it matters: reduces routing to finding one chokepoint. (awaits verification)
## Foundations & Systems
- Provenance — Every claim carries a traceable record of where it came from and how it was derived. Why it matters: makes the whole lattice auditable rather than trust-based. (awaits verification)
- Morphogenesis — Simple local rules can reliably grow into stable large-scale patterns. Why it matters: explains how structure self-organizes without a central blueprint. (awaits verification)
- Optimal transport — There is a cheapest way to reshape one distribution into another. Why it matters: gives a principled distance between shapes, data, and states. (awaits verification)
- Safety-case — A claim of safety must be backed by an explicit, checkable argument, not assertion. Why it matters: turns "it's safe" into something you can inspect and challenge. (awaits verification)
- Fixpoint domain — Self-referential definitions settle on a well-defined stable meaning. Why it matters: lets systems define themselves without circular collapse. (awaits verification)
- Sheaf cohomology — Measures the gap between what is true locally everywhere and what can be stitched into one global truth. Why it matters: obstruction to gluing is itself a precise, computable object. (awaits verification)
- Entropy dynamics — Closed systems drift toward disorder, and that drift sets an arrow of time. Why it matters: explains irreversibility and the cost of maintaining structure. (awaits verification)
- Koszul duality — Two seemingly different structures encode exactly the same information, each recoverable from the other. Why it matters: lets a hard problem be swapped for an equivalent easier one. (awaits verification)
- Game-diagonalization bridge — Fixed-point theorems prove things must exist; diagonalization proves things cannot. Why it matters: together they map the exact boundary between what is provable and what is impossible. (awaits verification)
- Info-theoretic belief entropy — Uncertainty in a belief can be measured like information, and updating beliefs has a quantifiable cost. Why it matters: turns evidence-weighing into precise bookkeeping. (awaits verification)
- Pisot number — A special number greater than one whose powers snap ever closer to whole numbers. Why it matters: gives a concrete source of self-organizing near-integer structure and quasi-periodic order. (awaits verification)
- Poincare recurrence — In a closed system that conserves volume, almost every state eventually returns arbitrarily close to where it started. Why it matters: guarantees long-run return without needing to track the path. (awaits verification)
- Birkhoff ergodic theorem — For well-mixed systems, the long-run time average equals the average over all states. Why it matters: lets one trajectory stand in for the whole space. (awaits verification)
- Homology — Counts the essential holes in a shape in a way that survives bending and stretching. Why it matters: gives robust invariants that tell different shapes apart. (awaits verification)
- Ergodic decomposition — Any invariant system splits uniquely into indivisible well-mixed pieces. Why it matters: reduces complex long-run behavior to simple building blocks. (awaits verification)
- Lie algebra — Captures the local infinitesimal symmetry of a smooth continuous group as simple bracket algebra. Why it matters: turns hard curved symmetry problems into linear ones. (awaits verification)
- Northcott property — Only finitely many points sit below any fixed bound on height and degree. Why it matters: guarantees finiteness, making infinite arithmetic searches actually terminate. (awaits verification)
- Bogomolov conjecture — Points of small height on a curve inside its Jacobian are finite unless the curve is special. Why it matters: sharpens Northcott finiteness into a precise geometric dichotomy. (awaits verification)
- Arakelov intersection — Extends geometric intersection numbers to include contributions from the infinite places, giving a single arithmetic measure. Why it matters: unifies geometry over finite and infinite primes into one height theory. (awaits verification)
- Beilinson-Bloch — Predicts that heights of algebraic cycles encode the leading behavior of L-functions. Why it matters: generalizes BSD from curves to higher-dimensional cycles. (awaits verification)
- Complex multiplication — Certain elliptic curves carry extra hidden symmetry that forces their special points to be arithmetically explicit. Why it matters: makes CM points computable landmarks underpinning André-Oort. (awaits verification)
